The short version. tdlib's protocol code is careful; its timers are not. Every call it lost, and every large lead we have, comes from five choices: a 135 s background timeout, a 2 s RTT floor, check mode, per-block AES decrypt and hanging up 1 s before a tunnel ends. Our engine wins wherever a link silently dies, tunnels or flaps, and on raw download speed.

Where we are weaker. On clean or merely slow links neither engine matters and tdlib is as good. We have no HTTP transport and no in-engine PFS, our uploads are unpaced, we copy received bytes twice as often, and our liveness logic is 79 constants tuned in a simulator we wrote ourselves.

tdlib, roasted

Each weakness below is proven twice: by the paired numbers, and by the lines in tdlib 1.8.49 that produce them. Where a cause was in doubt it was switched off in a diagnostic tdlib build and measured again.

tdlib · Background

W1 · A dead connection lives for 135 seconds

When a path dies without a reset (a NAT entry expires, a middlebox drops the flow, Wi-Fi hands over to a captive portal) no socket error ever arrives. With the app in the background, tdlib waits for its read timeout, and every call made in the meantime waits with it.

tdlib · Foreground

W2 · A 2-second RTT floor means 5 seconds to notice

Online, tdlib scales its timeouts with RTT, but rtt() never goes below 2 s, and the ping timeout is 2.5 × that. On a 50 ms link it still takes 5 s of silence (7 s for reads) before tdlib gives up on a connection. Its download and upload sessions are not is_main_, so they keep the 135 s ping timeout even in the foreground.

tdlib · Flaky links

W3 · Check mode: prove the server exists before every new connection

After any error on a DC address in the last 10 seconds, every new tdlib connection first runs a req_pq ping before it may carry a query, and at most three may be checking at once while queries wait. On a link that drops connections every few seconds, tdlib lives in check mode.

RoastOne bad packet marks the address, and for the next 10 seconds every connection spends a full round trip asking the server for a nonce it will throw away. The three-at-a-time cap then turns that round trip into a queue. It is a safety check written for broken DC lists, applied to every lost packet.

tdlib · Throughput

W4 · AES-IGE decrypt makes one OpenSSL call per 16 bytes

Every downloaded byte goes through IGE decryption. tdlib encrypts 31 blocks per EVP call using a CBC trick, but its decrypt loop calls EVP_DecryptUpdate once per 16-byte block. About 47 % of its IGE time is spent inside those calls rather than in AES itself.

tdlib · Tunnels

W5 · Hangs up a second before the tunnel ends

A train tunnel is 6 seconds of silence on a connection that would resume afterwards. tdlib's foreground ping timeout is 5 s, so it kills the connection about a second before the tunnel ends. New connections are refused inside the tunnel, and after it the online flood control spaces reconnects out. The 6 s gap becomes 11 s.

What our engine does instead

What tdlib did better, and we took

Two of tdlib's leads were real engineering, not noise. Both ideas are now in our engine; one of them is still only a tie.

Credit to tdlib

Test-compress a sample before gzipping a large request

Our engine used to gzip every large request, including random bytes from encrypted media. tdlib compresses a 1 KB sample from the middle first and skips the rest if it does not shrink. We copied it: upload CPU dropped from 0.14 s to 0.05 s in the same run.

Credit to tdlib

Ask for quick acks sparingly

tdlib asks the server for a quick ack only when a query asked for one. We asked on every large packet to time our upload grace, which cost one extra downlink frame per part: on a lossy link that frame queues ahead of the answers, and tdlib's uploads were 6–9 % faster in 5 of 5 rounds. Now we keep one quick ack in flight at a time: 26.3 s against tdlib's 26.1 s. That is a tie, not a win.

Us, roasted

The same treatment for our engine. Some of these cost calls, some only look bad, and two are features tdlib has and we do not.

Us · Costs calls

R1 · We measure our upload queue and then do nothing about it

Behind a bloated modem buffer, calls wait behind our own upload. tdlib does not pace uploads either, but its upload runs slower (21.6 s against our 21.1 s), so less of it sits in the buffer and its typical call comes out about 10 % faster. We already measure delivery rate per session and even track every session's unconfirmed upload bytes. We use that only to stretch other sessions' timeouts. Capping unconfirmed upload bytes at about two bandwidth-delay products would keep calls out of the queue.

Us · Ugly, measured free

R2 · Every received byte is copied four times

On the plain transport a chunk is copied into scratch, de-obfuscated, appended to decrypted_input, sliced out again by take() into a frame Vec, and the buffer compacts with drain. tdlib's ChainBuffer decrypts in place: 0.037 ms of memmove per MB against our 0.075. We cut two of the copies once (fused IGE decrypt, in-place CTR on append) and an 8 GB download went from 10.72 s to 10.78 s of CPU, so it was reverted. The code is still wasteful; the CPU does not care.

Us · Risk

R3 · Seventy-nine hand-tuned constants, tuned on a simulator we wrote

tdlib's whole liveness model is four formulas (section W1). Ours is 54 constants in session/mod.rs and 25 in session_runtime.rs: grace rates, fresh allowances, suspect holds, racer budgets, peak lifetimes, stretch limits. Each one was added to fix a scenario in mtproto-netsim, which models a network the way we imagined it (one token bucket per direction, stalls per 1400 bytes). Every win on this page was scored in that same simulator. tdlib's constants are crude, but years of real phones have tested them.

Us · Missing

R4 · No HTTP transport

tdlib can fall back to HTTP with long polling (http_wait) and its address picker considers HTTP options. On a network that passes only HTTP (some corporate proxies, captive networks) tdlib still connects. Our engine speaks TCP transports only, and a received http_wait is ignored (coverage row T22).

Us · Missing

R5 · Perfect forward secrecy is someone else's job

tdlib creates temporary keys and binds them with auth.bindTempAuthKey inside its own session code. Our engine does not: MtProtoKit's MTContext in the host app creates and binds every key, and the engine only reports the conditions. It is the one row marked gap in our 220-row protocol coverage table, and it means the engine cannot run on its own with PFS.

Us · Edge case

R6 · An acknowledged query that never gets an answer waits for the host

If the server acknowledges a query and then never answers it, tdlib closes the connection after 60 s and asks again. That does not resolve it either, but tdlib tries. Our engine relies on the host's request timeout. Unacknowledged queries are fully covered (retransmitted under the original msg_id, exactly once).

Us · No edge

R7 · Where the link is the bottleneck, it is a coin flip

59 of 122 pairs are within 10 %. In 13 of them tdlib is ahead on both the typical and the slowest call, by at most 2 % and 6 % (3g/mixed-upload, bufferbloat-down/mixed, bufferbloat-down/mixed-upload, bufferbloat/mixed, edge/steady, latency/perfect, …). Clean, merely slow or lossy links are limited by the link, not by the engine, and on those our timers buy nothing. The lossy upload we fixed is still 0.8 % slower than tdlib's, and on the clean-link download tdlib's slowest calls were 1.2× faster, though that run has only 3–4 calls during the transfer.

Us · Method

R8 · We built the stadium, the referee and the weather

The test server, the network simulator and the harness are ours. tdlib's ConnectionCreator needs the whole Td object, so its client_loop runs as a line-by-line replica rather than tdlib's own code. The first passes contained four harness bugs, and all four penalised tdlib: its server time difference started at 0, so every first call paid a bad_server_salt round trip; all its sessions shared one thread; the replica marked addresses faulty too often; and it used check mode through a proxy. All four are fixed in every number here, and a race against tdlib on the real servers has not been run, because the test account has 2FA and TDLib could not log in. Our engine is three days old.

Contenders

Our engine at 6b16c05, the commit in the app today. tdlib 1.8.49 (e894536b2), CMake Release, running its real Session, SessionConnection, RawConnection, obfuscated transport, fake-TLS TlsInit and flood controls.

Ground

mtproto-testserver behind mtproto-netsim on one Mac. Both engines got the same link seed in every paired run: latency, jitter, per-direction rate, stalls, resets, refused connects, blackholes, tunnels and shared bufferbloat queues.

Scoring

61 scenarios, each with the app in the foreground and in the background. "Leads" means more than 10 % better on the typical call, the slowest 1 % or throughput. The quick suite is the median of 3 paired rounds, the weak suite one paired run, and the lossy uploads the median of 5 rounds after the quick-ack change.

Not measured

Real Telegram servers against tdlib; memory beyond RSS (ours 3–6 MB, tdlib 11–13 MB); battery. A media/flaky row where tdlib's per-part latency is lower is a queue-share effect of netsim's single token bucket, not a tdlib strength: its whole download took 41.8 s against our 21.0 s.
